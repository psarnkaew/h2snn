                                                                                      Abstract
                                                     Heterogeneous graphs, whose nodes and edges may belong to different
                                                     types and feature spaces, arise in a wide variety of real-world domains
                                                     such as biology, chemistry and computer networks. Existing methods
                                                     typically address this heterogeneity by modifying the model architecture
                                                     itself, which often results in specialized and parameter-intensive designs.
                                                     To address this issue, we propose HetSheaf, a framework that models
                                                     heterogeneous relational data through cellular sheaves, which provide a
                                                     principled topological framework for encoding type-specific local feature
                                                     spaces and their interactions directly in the data representation. We also
                                                     introduce a family of heterogeneous sheaf predictors that learn restriction
                                                     maps conditioned on node and edge types. To enable graph-level predictions,
                                                     we further propose SheafPool, a graph pooling mechanism that aggregates
                                                     node representations in stalk space while remaining invariant to local changes
                                                     of basis, ultimately enabling stalk-space graph-level representations for the
                                                     first time. HetSheaf achieves strong predictive performance on standard
                                                     heterogeneous graph benchmarks, over numerous tasks such as node/graph
                                                     classification, link prediction and recommendation, while reducing by up to
                                                     10x the number of parameters with respect to state-of-the-art baselines.


                                         1     Introduction
                                         Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) learn relational data by combining node features with
                                         graph structure through iterative message passing [6, 14, 27–29, 58, 65]. Their success
                                         has led to widespread adoption across a variety of domains such as biology, chemistry and
                                         computer networks, where entities and their interactions are naturally represented as graphs.
                                         While most standard GNNs are designed for homogeneous graphs, where all nodes and edges
                                         are assumed to belong to the same type and to be represented in a shared feature space,
                                         many real-world relational systems – for example, protein prediction [24], recommendation
                                         systems [18, 76], social network analysis [45, 56], and traffic prediction [31] – are best
                                         represented as heterogeneous graphs [15, 39, 75], with nodes and edges having different types
                                         and carrying features with different semantic meanings or dimensionalities. In these cases,
                                         GNNs struggle to process heterogeneous data mostly due to oversmoothing [48]. The latter
                                         refers to the case where neighboring nodes have increasingly similar representations. As the
                                         GNN becomes deeper, oversmoothing causes loss of type-specific features and information,
                                         thus significantly reducing the model’s performance [4, 7, 10].
                                         The key issue is that existing Heterogeneous GNNs (HGNNs) represent the data as a simple
                                         graph and attempt to account for the heterogeneity only in the model architecture [40, 59,
                                         68, 79]. Recent literature [2–4, 17] has demonstrated that attaching a cellular sheaf [12,

60] to a standard graph helps mitigate oversmoothing and increases the expressivity by
generalizing the diffusion process through a learned Sheaf Laplacian [36], which prevents
feature homogenization and captures complex, asymmetric edge relations. This makes sheaves
particularly well-suited for heterogeneous settings: restriction maps bridge distinct feature
spaces, allowing entities to interact while preserving type-specific semantics. Motivated by
this perspective, we introduce HetSheaf, a framework that learns a cellular sheaf encoding
the relevant type-specific information, by representing heterogeneous relational data through
a structure (fig. 1b) that explicitly preserves its local feature spaces and interactions. At
the same time, moving from graphs to sheaves also makes global aggregation and readout
more delicate. Because node representations now live in local vector spaces rather than in a
single common coordinate system, standard pooling strategies cannot be adopted naively. A
meaningful sheaf readout must account for the geometry of these stalk spaces. To address
this challenge we introduce SheafPool – a novel aggregation mechanism that explicitly
handles for these distinct geometries and ensures consistent global aggregation.
Contributions. Our main contributions can be summarised as follows:

     1. We propose HetSheaf, a general framework for heterogeneous sheaf neural networks
        that shifts the modelling of heterogeneity from architecture design to the underlying
        data representation through cellular sheaves.
     2. We demonstrate that Sheaf Graph Neural Networks provide an effective and
        parameter-efficient approach for heterogeneous learning, achieving competitive and
        often state-of-the-art performance across several benchmark datasets and tasks.
     3. We develop a family of heterogeneous sheaf predictors that incorporate node and
        edge type information directly into the prediction of restriction maps, yielding sheaf
        structures tailored to heterogeneous relational data.
     4. We propose SheafPool, a universal stalk-space readout for graph classification that
        is both permutation-invariant and gauge-invariant, making graph-level prediction
        with sheaf neural networks well defined and practically effective for the first time.
     5. We evaluate HetSheaf and SheafPool across a diverse suite of heterogeneous
        node classification, link prediction, and graph classification benchmarks. We achieve
        strong predictive performance and outperform both standard heterogeneous GNNs
        and type-agnostic sheaf models, while using up to 10x fewer parameters than existing
        heterogeneous baselines.

2   Background and Related Work
Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks. A wide range of GNNs
have been proposed for homogeneous graphs [11, 13, 34, 42, 46, 67]. Although these models
can be directly applied to heterogeneous graphs by ignoring node and edge types, they are
not explicitly designed to capture the type-specific structure of heterogeneous relational
data. While in homogeneous graphs all nodes/edges encode the same type of data, in
Heterogeneous GNNs, instead, nodes and edges can represent different types of information
in heterogeneous graphs. Several HGNNs have been proposed: a specific typology of them,
use meta-paths [62, 63], i.e., pre-defined node or edge type patterns that aim to capture
important semantic information. HAN [68] employs a hierarchical attention mechanism to
capture node-level importance among nodes and semantic-level importance across meta-paths.
MAGNN [25] combines several meta-path encoders to aggregate all information along the
meta-path. Both HAN and MAGNN require meta-paths to be generated manually in advance.
Graph Transformer Networks (GTNs) [77] automate this process by learning meta-paths
with graph transformation layers. However, Meta-paths are computationally expensive,
making them infeasible for graphs with many distinct edge types. Instead, the graph may be
treated as a relational graph. RSHN [83] constructs a coarsened line graph to generate edge
features, and then applies a Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) [26] to propagate
node and edge features. R-GCN [59] splits the heterogeneous graph into the subgraph
induced by each edge type, then applies a standard GCN to each subgraph. HGT [79] is a
graph transformer that can process large heterogeneous graphs using subgraph sampling.
HetGNN [79] uses random walks to sample strongly correlated heterogeneous neighbours and


                                              2
then applies an RNN for representation learning. SlotGAT [81] uses a slot-based message
passing approach with an attention mechanism for aggregation. SimpleHGNN [48], applies a
series of additional techniques to lift GATs to heterogeneous domains and Space4HGNN [80]
defines a unified design space of modular components for heterogeneous GNNs and allows
exhaustive evaluation of many combinations of techniques. More recently, HAGNN [82]
meta-path-based intra-type aggregation with meta-path-free inter-type aggregation through
a fused meta-path graph, while HGNN-AR2 [47] adaptively reconstructs relations to address
same-category connection deficiencies in meta-path methods. Instead of introducing a new
architecture for heterogeneous graphs, we propose shifting from representing heterogeneous
relational data as graphs to cellular sheaves, which preserve all type-specific information and
enable powerful processing with sheaf-specific tools.
Sheaf Neural Networks. Standard GNNs are known to suffer from limitations such
as oversmoothing [7], oversquashing [1] and degradation of performances in heterophilic
settings, i.e., when adjacent nodes belong to different classes or carry contrasting attributes.
Cellular sheaf theory [12, 60] equips graphs with local feature spaces, or stalks, together
with linear restriction maps along edges, thereby providing a principled geometric framework
for modelling and alleviating these pathologies. Sheaf Neural Networks were originally
introduced using an hand-crafted sheaf with a single dimensionality in [35], and further
improved by allowing to learn the sheaf using a learnable parametric function in Bodnar et al.
[4]. Since then, sheaf-based graph learning has been extended in several directions: attention
mechanisms over sheaves [2], connection Laplacians learned during preprocessing [3], sheaf-
based positional encodings [37], nonlinear diffusion processes [78], joint diffusion biases [8],
polynomial spectral filtering [5], and directional extensions [23]. Beyond standard graphs,
the sheaf framework has also been extended to hypergraphs [17, 51], recommendation
systems [55], federated learning [52], cooperative sheaf dynamics [57], and more general
topological constructions via copresheaves [32]. Existing sheaf neural networks have focused
almost exclusively on node-level tasks, leaving graph-level prediction largely unexplored.
Indeed, graph-level readout in sheaf networks is more subtle than standard aggregation,
since a valid pooling operator must not only be permutation-invariant over nodes, but also
invariant to node-wise stalk reparameterisations. Existing graph pooling methods [30, 41,
43, 46] satisfy the former requirement but not the latter, and therefore cannot be directly
applied in a principled way to sheaf representations.

model heterogeneity through architecture-level components such as meta-path extractors or
relation-specific message passing. (b) HetSheaf, instead, captures heterogeneity directly in

downstream decoder for graph classification.

3.1   HetSheaf : A Sheaf-based framework for Heterogeneous data

Existing heterogeneous GNNs (Figure 1a) handle heterogeneity at the architectural level
often leading to increasingly specialised and parameter-heavy designs, scaling with the
number of entity types and making the treatment of heterogeneity tightly coupled to the
neural architecture itself. For this reason, we instead take a representation-centric view by
modifying the input structure to encode specific heterogeneous knowledge while maintaining
topological information. Unlike standard graphs, which assume a single shared feature space
for all nodes, cellular sheaves naturally capture heterogeneous data through type-specific
local vector spaces and restriction maps that preserve local structure while enabling coherent
global message passing. Motivated by this perspective, we introduce HetSheaf(Figure 1b),
a general framework for heterogeneous sheaf neural networks. Given a heterogeneous graph
G = (V, E, ϕ, ψ), it first preprocesses type-dependent node features into a common channel
dimension, then infers a cellular sheaf whose restriction maps are conditioned on both
features and type information, and finally applies a sheaf neural architecture for downstream
prediction. More formally, a heterogeneous sheaf neural network consists of three components:

      1. Type-aware preprocessing, which projects type-specific input features into a shared
         channel space. Indeed, as each node type may have a different number of feature
         channels, we use a linear layer with bias for each node type to project the node
         features to the same number of input channels. This can be combined with the sheaf
         projection step of the SheafGNN to create the sheaf cochain x ∈ C 0 (G, F). The
         modified linear layer takes in the original feature channels f0 and outputs df features
         as the node stalk projection. We follow [48, 81] to generate the input feature types
         by using either all of the node feature types (type-0), only the feature of the node
         target type (type-1) or replacing all node features as one-hot vectors (type-2).
      2. Heterogeneous sheaf inference, (Figure 2) which predicts restriction maps conditioned
         on node features and type information.
      3. Sheaf-based representation learning, which applies a sheaf neural encoder followed
         by a task-specific decoder or readout. Similar to Lv et al. [48], we perform ℓ2

5     Experimental Evaluation
We evaluate HetSheaf on a diverse suite of heterogeneous node-classification, link-prediction,
and graph-classification task benchmarks, and complement the main results with ablations
and architectural analyses. We study the impact of heterogeneous sheaf predictors, different
restriction-map families, attention-based sheaf constructions, and the proposed universal
stalk-space late-token readout for graph-level prediction. We also assess the model-agnosticity
of the framework by instantiating heterogeneous sheaves over multiple sheaf architectures [2,
4, 5], and we analyse the associated computational overhead and parameter efficiency.
Altogether, these experiments test four claims: (i) encoding heterogeneity directly in the
inferred sheaf yields strong performance across node-, edge-, and graph-level tasks; (ii)
explicitly conditioning restriction maps on typed information improves over type-agnostic
sheaf learning and remains competitive with specialised heterogeneous GNN baselines; (iii)
HetSheaf is model-agnostic, enabling multiple sheaf architectures to operate on the same
heterogeneous sheaf representation without ad hoc redesign; and (iv) these gains are achieved
with favourable parameter efficiency, while preserving typed geometric structure through a
gauge-aware graph-level readout.
Datasets, Baselines and Evaluation Protocol. We rely on a broad collection of hetero-
geneous benchmarks [19, 20, 38, 40, 44, 48–50, 68, 70, 74, 81] spanning node classification,
link prediction, and graph classification. The suite covers citation graphs (e.g., DBLP, ACM),
knowledge graphs (e.g., IMDB), user-item interaction networks (e.g., LastFM, MovieLens,
Amazon, YouTube), link prediction graphs (e.g., PubMed), biological graphs (e.g., PRO-
TEINS, ENZYMES), chemical compounds (e.g., MUTAG, NCI1), biomedical networks
(e.g., CTD-DDA, DrugBank, NDFRT-DDA, String-PPI), and networking tasks (e.g., AS773,
RouteNet Traffic). We compare against a representative set of homogeneous, heterogeneous,
and sheaf-based baselines, namely GCN [42], GAT [66], GIN [71], GraphSAGE [33], R-
GCN [59], HAN [68], HGT [40], and Neural Sheaf Diffusion [4]. Unless otherwise stated, we
follow the standard protocols associated with each benchmark, using the HGB evaluation
setup [48]. For node and graph classification, we report both Macro-F1 and Micro-F1,
while for link prediction we report AUPR and AUROC. Negative edges for link prediction
are generated following prior work on heterogeneous relation prediction [9, 25, 79]. Model
selection is performed through large-scale hyperparameter sweeps, and we report the best
validation-selected configuration for each model. Detailed dataset descriptions, preprocessing
choices, additional baseline comparisons from the literature, hyperparameter search spaces,
hardware details, and other implementation notes are deferred to Sections B.1, B.3 and B.4.

5.1   Experimental Results
Table 2 reports the main results across tasks, where HetSheaf emerges as the most
consistently strong method. It achieves the best performance on all three node-classification
benchmarks, ranks first on the two largest recommendation-style link-prediction datasets,
and remains highly competitive on the remaining relation-prediction tasks. On graph
classification, where the challenge is no longer only to learn typed local interactions but also
to compress them into a stable graph-level representation, HetSheaf again performs strongly.

aspects of the model: local typed discrimination, relation-aware compatibility, and global

Section B.3 situates HetSheaf within a broader landscape of heterogeneous GNNs from the
recent literature. Finally, Sections A and B.4 provides a complementary systems-level view,
including runtime and parameter-count comparisons, showing that the empirical gains are
obtained with modest computational overhead. Taken together, the results support three
conclusions. First, explicitly encoding heterogeneity in the inferred sheaf yields systematic

6     Conclusion

In this work, we presented HetSheaf, a sheaf-agnostic framework for heterogeneous sheaf
neural networks. Rather than handling heterogeneity through increasingly specialised
architectures, HetSheaf models it directly at the representation level through cellular
sheaves, enabling a principled treatment of type-specific local feature spaces and interactions.
We introduced a family of heterogeneous sheaf predictors that explicitly condition restriction
maps on node and edge type information, yielding sheaf structures tailored to heterogeneous
relational data. To support graph-level learning, we further proposed SheafPool, a universal
stalk-space late-token readout that is permutation-invariant and robust to local stalk gauge
transformations, thereby making graph classification in sheaf networks both well defined and
practically effective for the first time. Across a broad set of benchmarks, HetSheaf delivers
strong, often state-of-the-art, predictive performance while remaining substantially more
parameter-efficient than competing heterogeneous GNN baselines. Because the framework
is compatible with existing sheaf architectures, it provides a general foundation for future
progress in heterogeneous sheaf learning, and its underlying principles extend naturally
beyond graphs to hypergraphs and more general topological domains.

A     Universal Stalk-Space Late-token Readout: Formalization and
      Properties
This appendix provides a formal treatment of our graph-level readout for sheaf neural
networks. We proceed in four steps. First, we formalize graph classification in the presence
of local stalk gauges and explain why standard flatten-and-pool readouts are not well-defined
in this setting. Second, we derive the proposed Universal Stalk-Space Late-token Readout
and prove its permutation invariance and gauge compatibility under natural identifiability
assumptions. Third, we compare the proposed late, receive-only token to the classification
token used in Vision Transformers (ViTs), clarifying why the latter cannot be imported
naively into sheaf encoders. Finally, following the multiset perspective used in expressive
GNN theory, we state and prove an injective universal readout theorem on the canonicalized
stalk descriptor space. This theorem formalizes that injective graph-level pooling is possible
in principle after gauge canonicalization and our practical late-token energy head should be
viewed as a structured, low-overhead instantiation of this broader family.

A.1    Problem Setting and Gauge Quotient Viewpoint
Let Gg = (Vg , Eg ) be a graph and let a sheaf neural encoder produce node embeddings:
                                  Hi ∈ Rd×C for i ∈ Vg                                    (6)
where d is the stalk dimension and C is the channel dimension. For graph classification, the
goal is to construct a readout that summarizes the graph into a single graph representation:
                                  R : {Hi }i∈Vg 7→ gg ∈ RF                                (7)

In ordinary GNNs, node embeddings live in a common ambient space (shared latent space)
RC , so a graph representation can be obtained by applying any permutation-invariant pooling
operator to the multiset {hi }i∈Vg . In a sheaf model, by contrast, the node state at i belongs
to the local stalk F(i), and only becomes a matrix Hi ∈ Rd×C after choosing a basis of F(i).
Since there is in general no canonical basis shared across nodes, the coordinates Hi are not
intrinsic: if the basis of F(i) is changed by Gi ∈ Gi ⊆ GL(d), then the same underlying stalk
signal is represented by:
                                Hi 7→ H′i := Gi Hi with Gi ∈ G                              (8)
where G depends on the restriction-map family: a diagonal subgroup of GL(d) in the diagonal
case, O(d) or a metric-preserving subgroup in the orthogonal/bundle case, and GL(d) in the
general case. We refer to this action as a local gauge action on the stalk.
  Definition 1 (Gauge Orbit). For a node embedding H ∈ Rd×C , define its gauge orbit as:
                                     [H]G := {GH : G ∈ G}                                  (9)
 A graph-level readout is gauge-invariant if it depends only on the multiset of node-wise
 gauge orbits.

Thus, a principled sheaf readout must satisfy two requirements simultaneously:
     1. Permutation invariance over nodes: reordering the nodes of a graph must not
        change the output.
     2. Gauge compatibility / invariance: equivalent local coordinate realizations of
        the same stalk signal must produce the same graph descriptor.

A.2    Flatten-and-Pool fails for Sheaf Neural Networks
In sheaf neural networks, since each node representation Hi ∈ Rd×C lives in a local stalk space,
two tensors that represent the same underlying local signal may differ by a transformation of
the form Hi 7→ Gi Hi , with Gi taken from the relevant gauge group. Any pooling rule that
acts directly on the raw coordinates of Hi therefore risks depending on arbitrary local frame
choices rather than on the intrinsic sheaf signal itself. This issue is particularly important
after flattening. Indeed, vectorizing Hi identifies the stalk and channel axes with a single
ambient Euclidean coordinate system, thereby implicitly assuming that all nodes are already
expressed in a common stalk basis. But this assumption is precisely what fails in the sheaf
setting: different nodes may carry equivalent information in different local coordinates. As a
result, applying a standard graph readout to vec(Hi ) ∈ RdC mixes together quantities that
are not canonically comparable across nodes.
From a geometric viewpoint, flatten-and-pool collapses the quotient structure induced by
the local gauge action. A correct sheaf readout should should first construct canonical
representatives of these classes before aggregation and naive flattening does not do that.
Therefore, even if the encoder has learned meaningful stalk-structured node states, a basis-
dependent readout may destroy this structure at the final stage and produce graph embeddings
that vary under purely representational changes.
  Proposition 1 (Flatten-and-pool is not gauge-invariant). Let vec(·) denote vectorization.
  Consider any readout that pools flattened node embeddings through a coordinate-wise operator
  such as sum, mean, max, or standard attention on vec(Hi ) ∈ Rd×C . Then, this readout is
  not invariant under node-wise gauge transformations Hi 7→ Gi Hi .

 Proof. Using the standard Kronecker identity, we have that:
                                 vec(GH) = (IC ⊗ G) vec(H)                                 (12)
 Hence, under a node-wise gauge transform we obtain:
                                 vec(H′i ) = (IC ⊗ Gi ) vec(Hi )                           (13)
 If we consider, for instance, sum pooling, we get:

 Max pooling is not equivariant under general invertible linear maps, and thus coordinate-

The proposition shows that one must either pool purely gauge-invariant node descriptors or
first canonicalize local stalk frames before pooling in stalk space. The former is robust but
collapses the stalk structure too early, while the latter is the route we adopt.

A.3    Design Principle: Canonicalize and Pool
A valid readout for sheaf graph classification should satisfy the following desiderata: it must
support graph-level prediction, it must remain meaningful for nontrivial stalk dimension


                                                19
d > 1, it must remain valid across diagonal, orthogonal, and general restriction-map families
and it should preserve stalk-structured information as long as possible before the final graph
compression.
We have seen that the natural alternatives are indeed insufficient. First, standard pooling
on flattened embeddings is computationally simple but fails Proposition 1. Second, direct
stalk-space pooling with energy-based weights is valid only if the residual gauge is orthogonal,
and therefore does not extend to the general GL(d) case. Third, invariant-only pooling, for
example pooling per-node channel energies, is universal but collapses the stalk dimension
before cross-node interactions in stalk space can be exploited.
Our solution is a two-stage canonicalization followed by stalk-space aggregation. We first
use per-node whitening to remove the non-orthogonal part of the local gauge. We then use
anchor-based orthogonal Procrustes alignment to express all nodes in a common stalk frame.
Only after this canonicalization, we pool in Rd×C . A late, receive-only token is then used to
add graph-level expressive capacity without altering the encoder dynamics. The proposed
Universal Stalk-Space Late-token Readout has already been described in Section 4, but now
we are going to see it again together with mathematical derivations and proofs.

A.5      Classification Token in Vision Transformers Comparison
In order to better understand the role of the late token, we can make a comparison with
classification token ([CLS]) in Vision Transformers[16], even if the two mechanisms play
fundamentally different roles.
The reason for this difference is structural: while in a ViT, all patch tokens already live in a
single global embedding space, so a global token may interact with them directly throughout
the encoder, in sheaf neural networks, node states live in local stalk spaces and are only
defined up to node-wise basis changes. Hence a token cannot be inserted meaningfully into
the encoder without first resolving or otherwise respecting the stalk gauge. Moreover, in


                                                 23
graph message passing, inserting a token node connected to all nodes changes the propagation
graph itself. This effectively introduces global shortcuts, reduces the effective graph diameter,
and may accelerate oversmoothing by forcing repeated mixing through a global hub. Our
token is therefore intentionally late and receive-only.
  Proposition 3 (The late token does not alter encoder propagation). Let Eθ denote the
  sheaf encoder, so that:
                                      {Hi }i∈Vg = Eθ (Gg , Xg )                             (54)
  Let Rω denote the late-token readout. Then the full model factors as:
                                   ybg = Dη ◦ Rω ◦ Eθ (Gg , Xg )                            (55)
 and the encoder mapping Eθ is independent of the late token parameters.
  Proof. By construction, the late token is introduced only after the encoder has already
  produced {Hi }i∈Vg . It is therefore not part of the message passing graph and does not enter
  any propagation, diffusion, or attention update inside Eθ . Consequently the propagation
  operator of the encoder is unchanged.
 Remark 3. In this sense, our token is closer to a post-encoder set summarization token
 than to a standard transformer class token. Its job is not to mediate message passing, but
 to add expressive readout capacity after gauge-compatible aggregation has already taken
 place.

A.7    Graph-level Readout Overhead

For graph classification, the sheaf diffusion backbone is followed by a graph-level readout
(pooling) step that aggregates stalk-space node representations into a single graph embedding.
We consider five readout strategies: three parameter-free reductions (Mean, Max, Add),
an attention-weighted pool, and a late-interaction token pool. Table 3 reports the parameter
complexity of each readout using the same notation as table 25: n nodes, stalk dimension d,
feature channels f , c = df , output classes K, and attention heads h.


                                               27
Table 3: Parameter complexity of graph-level readout strategies. Notation follows
table 25: stalk dimension d, channels f , c = df , output classes K, attention heads h.
Readout                   Parameters                          Computational complexity
Mean / Max / Add          K(c + 1)                            O(Kc + nc)
Attention                 c(c + h + 1) + K(c + 1)             O(c2 + nc)
Late token                2df + 4f 2 + 6f + 1 + K(f + 1)      O(f 2 + nf )


The parameter-free reductions (Mean, Max, Add) introduce only the final linear classifier
mapping from Rc to RK , contributing O(Kc) parameters. The runtime cost is dominated
by the O(nc) global summation over the node set. Because K is typically small (equal
to the number of classes), this overhead is negligible relative to the diffusion layers. The
attention readout adds a gating network that computes per-node importance scores before
aggregation; its dominant parameter cost is O(c2 ), arising from the query and key projections,
and therefore scales quadratically with c rather than with any graph-level quantity. The
late-token readout, which appends a learnable [graph] token and processes it through a
cross-attention layer, exhibits a similar O(f 2 ) scaling in the feature dimension.
Crucially, in all five cases the readout cost depends exclusively on the model hyperparameters
(d, f , K, h) and is independent of the number of edges m, up to the O(n) summation
inherent in any global pooling. Because the sheaf diffusion layers themselves already scale as
O(mc) or O(md2 c) per layer (table 25), and m ≫ 1 for all benchmarks considered in this
work, the readout overhead is dominated by the diffusion cost, making the choice of pooling
strategy effectively free from a computational standpoint.

B.2.5   Effect of Restriction Map Type
The predictor ablations also reveal a second important axis: the choice of restriction-map
family. Across tasks, no single family dominates universally. Polynomial families are often
strongest on node classification and on several graph classification benchmarks, where
longer-range typed interactions are beneficial. By contrast, non-polynomial diagonal or
bundle families can be preferable on more semantically heterogeneous or noisier datasets
such as IMDB and ENZYMES. This task dependence is itself informative: it suggests that
the optimal local transport geometry depends on whether the problem rewards smooth
long-range propagation, local typed alignment, or robust inductive generalisation.
For node classification, Diag-Poly and Gen.-Poly are often strongest on ACM and DBLP,
whereas non-polynomial families dominate on IMDB. For link prediction, the restriction-map
family tends to have a smaller effect, with diagonal maps performing consistently well
and general maps providing only modest gains in some settings. For graph classification,
polynomial families are essential on some datasets but can also become unstable on others,
especially when small graph size and structural diversity interact poorly with higher-order
spectral filtering. This reinforces the importance of treating the restriction-map family as a
genuine modelling choice rather than as a fixed default.

B.2.6   Attention-based Restriction Map Variants
Tables 13–15 repeat the main ablation study using attention-based restriction-map con-
structions. These experiments test whether replacing the standard predictor output by a
normalised attention-style map provides a useful inductive bias.
For node classification (Table 13), attention-based variants broaden the set of competitive
configurations and occasionally outperform their non-attention counterparts, especially when
paired with bundle or general maps. For link prediction (Table 14), attention predictors
are competitive but do not provide a uniform improvement, suggesting that the advantage
of additional normalisation depends strongly on the relation structure of the dataset. For
graph classification (Table 15), the picture is mixed: attention expands the space of vi-
able configurations, but does not yield a systematic gain over the standard construction.
Overall, attention-based restriction maps are a useful modelling option, but not a universal
replacement.

B.2.7   Stalk Dimension Ablation Study
Tables 16–18 report the effect of varying the stalk dimension d ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5} across all
restriction map families, tasks, and datasets. Each cell reports Macro-F1 / Micro-F1 for
classification tasks and AUPR / AUROC for link prediction. Several patterns emerge.
First, no single stalk dimension is universally optimal: the best d varies by dataset and
family, suggesting that d should be treated as a hyperparameter tuned per task. For instance,
Gen.-P peaks at d = 2 on both ACM (94.3/94.2) and DBLP (95.0/95.4), but at d = 3 on
PROTEINS (80.6/82.1) and d = 2 on NCI1 (79.5/79.5).
Second, polynomial variants (Diag-P, Bundle-P, Gen.-P) consistently match or outperform
their non-polynomial counterparts on node and graph classification. Gen.-P achieves the

For graph classification, the depth–performance relationship is noisier, reflecting the smaller
test sets and inductive evaluation protocol. On ENZYMES, deeper sheaf models consistently
outperform shallower ones: Bundle achieves 62.3/63.3 at L = 8 (its global best) versus

78.2/78.2 at L = 3, both substantially above the homogeneous baselines (GCN: 64.2/65.0,
GAT: 64.2/65.0 at best). The polynomial families exhibit greater variance across layers than
the non-polynomial families: on NCI1, Diag-P ranges from 61.4 to 62.1, whereas Diag ranges
from 61.8 to 78.2. This is consistent with the observation from the stalk dimension ablation
that polynomial spectral filtering amplifies sensitivity to hyperparameter choices.
For link prediction (Table 21), all sheaf families are effectively invariant to depth. On
LastFM, AUPR varies by at most 0.2 percentage points across layers for all non-polynomial
families, and AUROC varies by at most 0.3 points. On MovieLens, performance is near-
constant across all depths and families (AUPR ≥ 99.5 and AUROC ≥ 99.4 for every sheaf
configuration), substantially outperforming HAN which is essentially non-functional on this
dataset (AUPR ≈ 63.0 regardless of depth). On Amazon and YouTube, the baselines are
more competitive, with HGT achieving the strongest baseline AUPR on both datasets (96.2
and 95.0 respectively), but the sheaf models match or exceed these scores across all layer
depths.
Overall, these results demonstrate that the sheaf Laplacian diffusion process provides a
principled mechanism for deep message passing without the oversmoothing pathology that
limits standard GNNs to shallow architectures. The restriction maps provide per-edge
transport operators that preserve discriminative signal across layers, while the polynomial
variants add spectral filtering that can exploit deeper receptive fields when the graph structure
supports it. In practice, we recommend treating L as a hyperparameter tuned alongside d
and the restriction map family, with the expectation that L ∈ {3, . . . , 6} providing a robust
default range.

B.4.1    Hardware and Software Setup
All experiments were conducted on an AWS EC2 instance running Ubuntu 24.04 LTS,
equipped with an NVIDIA L4 GPU (CUDA 12.8, cuDNN 9.1). The codebase is implemented
in Python 3.13.2 using PyTorch 2.8 and PyTorch Geometric 2.7 [21, 22], with dependency
management handled by uv11 . Hyperparameter searches were performed via Weights &
Biases12 sweeps. These choices were kept fixed across models to ensure that empirical
differences reflect modelling choices rather than software-stack variation.

B.4.2    Downstream Decoders and Loss Functions
The sheaf encoder produces node-level hidden states that are then consumed by a task-specific
decoder. Since node classification, link prediction, and graph classification place different
demands on the output space, we use standard downstream decoders tailored to each task
while leaving the sheaf backbone unchanged.

Node classification. After the Sheaf GNN processes the graph, the final node embeddings
are fed into an MLP to generate the classification logits. For multiclass tasks, we use a

B.4.3    Restriction Map Parameterisations
Each restriction map is represented as a d × d block matrix and parameterised by the
matrix-valued function Φ(xu , xv , ϕ(u), ϕ(v), ψ(e)). We briefly describe how each family is
constructed.
Diagonal restriction maps. The predictor outputs d values, which define the diagonal
entries of the restriction map.
General restriction maps. The predictor outputs d2 values, which are reshaped into a
full d × d matrix.
Orthogonal restriction maps. For orthogonal restriction maps F·⊴· ∈ O(d), we consider
two parameterisations. The first uses Householder products: the MLP outputs ⌊ d(d−1)     2   ⌋
parameters, which are converted by the Torch Householder library [53] into a product of
Householder reflections covering the full orthogonal group O(d). The second uses the Cayley
transform: the MLP outputs d2 values forming a matrix F ′ , from which the restriction map
is obtained as
                     F·⊴· = (Id − S)(Id + S)−1 ,       S = 21 (F ′ − F ′⊤ ).              (86)
Since the Cayley transform maps skew-symmetric matrices to SO(d), it can represent
rotations but not reflections. In practice, the Householder and Cayley parameterisations
provide different geometric biases, and the latter is the default for the attention predictors
and SheafAN variant following Barbero et al. [2].

B.4.4    Full Description of Heterogeneous Sheaf Predictors
We next give the complete definition of the heterogeneous predictor family used in the
experiments. These variants differ in two ways: first, in which typed signals are provided to
the predictor (node features, node types, edge types, or combinations thereof), and second,
in how this information is routed (shared predictor versus edge-type-specific ensemble). This
family lets us disentangle the contribution of feature-level conditioning from the contribution
of pure structural type information.
Let G be a heterogeneous graph with node features xu ∈ Rf , one-hot node-type vector eϕ(u) ,
and one-hot edge-type vector eψ(e) for edge e := (u, v). All predictors below are compatible
with diagonal, orthogonal, and general restriction-map types.
The predictors are broadly categorised by whether they append type embeddings to node
features, discard features in favour of pure type signals, or use specialised routing through
edge-type-specific experts.
